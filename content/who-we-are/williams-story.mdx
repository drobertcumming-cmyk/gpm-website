# Why I left the industry to build Grace.

An account from William Armour, Co-Founder and CEO of Grace Precious Metals.

I am a pastor. I spent a decade in the Gold IRA industry as a senior salesperson before I helped found Grace. The business I worked inside did not put its pricing where the customer could see it. What customers actually paid, once the markup was measured against the spot price of the metal on the day, was typically between a fifth and a third more than they understood themselves to be paying. For a long time I told myself I was an honest operator inside a difficult industry. What undid the story was a particular kind of customer — typically older, often widowed, who did her research, asked good questions, and was sold a higher-margin product in a way that was not false but was not plain. I talked to my pastor. He asked me the question I had not asked myself. I left. Grace is the company I built instead. The full account is below.

## The testimony

There were ten years of my professional life inside that industry. I was a senior salesperson in the Gold IRA business before I helped found Grace. I am also a pastor. For a long time I told myself the two roles could stay true at the same time. Eventually I could not tell myself that any more. This is the account of how I got there.

I came into the industry believing what most people who come in believe. Gold in a retirement account is a reasonable thing to offer people. There is a real market for it, a real regulatory framework around it, and real customers — often retirees — who have decided that a portion of their savings belongs in something other than paper. I thought I was in a business that helped those people do a thing they had already decided they wanted to do.

What I learned, slowly, is that the business I was in was not quite that business.

The business I worked inside did not put pricing where the customer could see it. The customer did not see a spread on a website, did not see a worked example on their rollover amount, and did not, in most cases, see a percentage during the phone call. What they saw was a price per coin, quoted by an advisor, for products that were selected for them. The markup inside that price, compared to the spot price of the metal on the day, was typically much larger than the customer understood themselves to be paying. Across the products in the catalogue, the markup varied, and the variation was not arbitrary — the products the advisor was most likely to steer the customer toward carried higher markups than the products the customer had initially asked about. When the round-trip cost was eventually calculated — entry markup, held period, buyback — customers had typically paid between a fifth and a third more than the metal was worth.

I want to be careful here. I am not saying every company in this category operates that way. I am saying the practice is widespread enough that it has produced documented enforcement actions, substantial customer losses in the aggregate, and a steady flow of elderly customers who discovered, years later, that the round-trip cost of the decision they made was nothing like what they had been led to understand.

For a while I told myself this was the industry, not me. I was honest. I did not lie to people. I gave them good information. I was a senior salesperson because I was a thoughtful one, and I believed that meant I was one of the better operators in a category that had some bad operators in it. That is a story you can tell yourself for a long time.

What undid the story, for me, was the kind of customer I saw repeatedly. She was typically older. Sometimes she was widowed. She had brought either her retirement account or, in some cases, her non-retirement savings. She had done the research she knew how to do. She asked good questions. She was offered a product that was a higher-margin product than the standard bullion she had initially asked about, and the product was explained to her in a way that was not false but was not plain. She said yes. The transaction went through. The margin on the transaction was significant, and some of that margin flowed to the person who sold it to her. That person was not always me, but on some transactions it had been.

Some time later I had a conversation with my pastor. I was not confessing. I was asking a question. I asked him what he thought about the practices I was describing, and whether a Christian man could participate in them in good faith if he was personally honest and personally careful. My pastor asked me a different question back. He asked whether the customers I had just described would describe what had happened to them as a fair exchange, if they had seen the same numbers I had seen. I said no, they would not. He asked me what I thought my professional position was, given that answer. I understood the question.

It took me longer than it should have to leave. When I was ready, I left. For a time I thought I could be a better version of what was already there. I eventually concluded I could not. The structure of the compensation, the structure of the product catalogue, and the structure of the pricing disclosure were all arranged to produce the outcome I was trying to stop producing. An honest salesperson inside that structure was still inside that structure.

What I wanted was a business where the numbers were on the page before the phone rang. Where the person the customer spoke to was paid the same whether the customer bought or not. Where the catalogue did not include the products the industry uses to hide margin. Where the buyback paid the market price rather than a marked-down price that created a second margin on the way out. Where a careful customer who asked good questions could see the answers on a website and decide for herself, without needing to pass through a conversation with a person whose income depended on what she decided.

My brother Andrew is a qualified accountant. My friend Duncan is a marketing executive who had spent time inside the industry and had reached some of the same conclusions I had. The three of us began working on what a company with those commitments would look like. Grace Precious Metals is that company.

The spread is 11.1%, all-in. No administration fee. No setup fee. The buyback is at the spot price on the day, never below. The product catalogue is standard IRS-eligible bullion — no numismatic coins, no rare coins, no exclusive coins, no proof coins. The advisors are salaried, and the salary is the whole compensation. The pricing is on the website, and the worked examples are on the pricing page. A customer who wants to understand what they will pay can understand what they will pay without speaking to anyone.

There is a verse, Proverbs 11:1, that reads *a false balance is an abomination to the Lord, but a just weight is his delight.* I did not choose the verse because it was inspirational. I chose it because it is the most exact description of what I understood, late, that I had been participating in. The false balance is the practice of weighing the gold on a scale the customer cannot see. The just weight is the practice of putting the scale on the counter where the customer can check it. The verse is operational. It describes a thing a business does or does not do. Grace does it. That is the whole pitch.

I am still a pastor. Grace is not a ministry, and I do not confuse the two. Grace is a business. It is a business organised around the idea that the thing I could not continue doing is not the only way the business can be run, and that there is a customer for the other way. Most of that customer base is made up of people like the ones I used to sell to: older, often Christian, often in the preservation phase of their working life, holding savings they cannot afford to lose to a markup they were not shown. I am building the company I would have wanted to send my own mother to, if she were making this decision, and had asked me where to go.

If you want to speak to one of our advisors, you can, and the conversation will not change the numbers you have already seen on this website.

That is the account. I am glad you read it.

— William
