# What is a buyback spread? A plain-English explanation.

The buyback spread is the gap between a gold dealer's buyback price and the current spot market price of gold. It is the dealer's margin on the exit side of your Gold IRA holding — the equivalent, in mirror image, of the markup you paid on entry.

## The mechanics

On the entry side, a dealer sells you gold at a price above the spot market price. The gap is the entry markup.

On the exit side — when you decide to sell the gold back — the dealer buys it from you at a price below the spot market price. The gap is the buyback spread.

Both spreads, added together, equal the round-trip cost of your holding: the total the dealer earned across the full cycle of your ownership.

## A simple example

Suppose the spot price of gold on the day you buy is $2,600 per ounce, and you buy 10 ounces. The dealer sells the gold at $2,900 per ounce (an entry markup of roughly 11.5% above spot). You pay $29,000.

Five years later, suppose the spot price on the day you sell is $3,400 per ounce. You sell the same 10 ounces back to the dealer. If the dealer buys back at spot, you receive $34,000. If the dealer buys back at a 10% buyback spread below spot, you receive $30,600 — $3,400 less than spot.

The dealer's total revenue across the round trip, in the second case, is the $3,000 entry markup plus the $3,400 buyback spread, for a total of $6,400 on your $29,000 holding. That is 22% of the original purchase price. It is also the number that was not visible to you at the point of purchase, because the entry-markup number alone was what you were quoted.

## Why it matters

The buyback spread is the single biggest reason why the total cost of a Gold IRA holding is often much higher than the entry-price number suggests. A customer who compared Gold IRA providers on entry markup alone, and who chose the provider with the lowest entry markup, may end up paying far more over the round trip than a customer who chose a provider with a higher entry markup but a narrower (or zero) buyback spread.

The buyback spread is, in this sense, the more important number — because the entry markup affects you once, at the point of purchase, while the buyback spread affects your entire exit, and the exit is when the money actually returns to you.

## How to find out what a provider's buyback spread is

Ask, in writing. Specifically, ask:

What is your buyback price per ounce today?

What is the spot reference price you are using?

What is the effective buyback spread (the gap between the two, as a percentage)?

Is this quote contractually guaranteed, or indicative only?

Does the buyback spread vary by product (bullion vs. premium vs. numismatic)?

If the provider will not put this information in writing, that is itself an answer.
