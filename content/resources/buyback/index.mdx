# How Gold IRA buyback works — and why the exit spread matters more than the entry spread.

Most Gold IRA customers focus, at the point of purchase, on the entry price. This is natural. The entry price is the number on the invoice, the figure the customer thinks about when they decide how much to roll over, the cost they mentally account for in the transaction. Entry price is load-bearing in the buying decision.

The exit price — what the customer receives when they eventually sell back — is typically not part of the buying decision at all. It is not shown at the point of purchase. It is not included in the marketing materials. It is sometimes not even known to the customer until the day they call to sell, at which point they discover a second number they were not told about. That number is the buyback spread, and for most customers who have held a Gold IRA for any length of time, the buyback spread ends up being the more financially consequential of the two.

This pillar explains how buyback works across the category, why the industry has structured buyback the way it has, what the documented patterns of harm look like, and how Grace's buyback-at-spot commitment is a structural response to the pattern.

## What "buyback" actually is

When a Gold IRA customer wants to exit — whether at retirement distribution, at a life-event liquidity need, or simply because they have decided to reallocate — the physical metals in their IRA need to be converted to cash. The cash is then either distributed to the customer (with whatever tax treatment applies to the distribution) or rolled into a different retirement account.

Two mechanisms produce the cash. The customer can take an in-kind distribution of the metals themselves, which triggers whatever tax treatment applies and leaves the customer personally holding the physical gold. More commonly, the customer sells the metals back to a dealer — typically the dealer who sold them in the first place — and receives cash. That sale is the "buyback."

The buyback price is the number the dealer offers to pay. It is quoted as a per-ounce figure, and it is compared, necessarily, to the current spot price of gold on the day of the transaction. The gap between the dealer's buyback price and the spot price is the buyback spread.

## The industry pattern

The category as a whole has historically structured buyback to generate a second margin on exit. The dealer's revenue on the original purchase was the entry markup. The dealer's revenue on the buyback is the buyback spread. Together, these two spreads produce the round-trip cost of the customer's original decision.

Industry-typical buyback spreads have, according to published research on the category and documented enforcement records, ranged from roughly 5% to 30% below spot depending on the dealer, the product category, and market conditions. On a standard bullion product — American Eagles, Canadian Maple Leafs — the typical buyback spread on exit tends to sit in a narrower band, but it is still meaningfully below spot. On a numismatic or "premium" product — proof coins, exclusive-release coins, certain labeled or graded coins — the buyback spread can be dramatically wider, because the dealer's original sale price was inflated above the underlying bullion value, and the buyback is marked to the bullion value rather than the original sale price.

The structural consequence: a customer who purchased a "premium" product at 30% above bullion value, and who later sells back at spot less a 10% buyback spread, has absorbed approximately 40% in combined spreads across the round trip. This is the arithmetic behind the documented pattern of consumer harm in the category, which enforcement records have quantified in hundreds of millions of dollars of aggregate consumer losses.

## Why the industry has structured it this way

The answer is not moral; it is structural. The Gold IRA category grew out of the broader precious metals dealer industry, which has historically operated on a "bid-ask" spread model — dealers quote a price to buy from customers (bid) and a price to sell to customers (ask), and the spread between the two is the dealer's margin. For a dealer operating in a physical-metal market with storage, logistics, and inventory costs, a bid-ask model is economically rational.

The problem, in the Gold IRA context, is that the customer is not comparing bid to ask in a single transaction. The customer is entering at the ask, holding for years, and exiting at the bid — and the bid-ask gap, compounded with any entry markup above market ask, becomes the round-trip cost of the retirement-account decision. When the customer does not see the bid (the buyback price) at the point of purchase, they cannot evaluate the round-trip cost. When the category does not publish the bid, the customer cannot shop on round-trip cost. When the bid is only revealed on the day of exit, the customer has no practical ability to act on the information.

This is the structural gap Grace is built to close.

## Grace's buyback: at spot, never below

Grace's buyback commitment is that we buy back at the spot price on the day. We do not mark the buyback down below spot to create a second margin on exit.

What this means for the customer: our revenue on your gold purchase is the entry spread — 11.1% of the purchase — and nothing else. There is no exit margin. If you purchase $100,000 of gold today at a spot price of $2,600 per ounce, and you sell back in five years at a spot price of $3,400 per ounce, you receive the spot price on the ounces you hold. Whatever the spot price is on that day, that is the number you receive.

The commercial consequence is that Grace's business economics are entirely in the entry spread. We do not have a second revenue pool on the way out. This is by design. It is the structural expression of the just weight: the weight the customer is told about at entry is the whole weight, and the exit is the market price without a hidden margin.

## How to test any Gold IRA provider's buyback policy

If you currently hold a Gold IRA, or if you are considering a Gold IRA and want to evaluate providers on the exit cost dimension, here is the test.

**Ask, in writing, for a buyback quote on the amount you hold or would purchase.** The quote should specify the price per ounce at which the provider would buy back, the spot price used as the reference, and the effective buyback spread. It should be timestamped.

**Ask whether the buyback quote structure is contractually guaranteed or indicative.** An indicative quote is subject to change at the point of exit. A contractually guaranteed buyback spread is a policy commitment.

**Ask whether the buyback applies to all products in the account or only to certain products.** Some providers apply different buyback spreads to bullion versus numismatic products, and the numismatic buyback can be dramatically wider than the bullion buyback.

**Ask how long the provider has been in business and whether buyback obligations are documented in writing as part of the customer relationship.** A dealer that goes out of business during your holding period complicates the exit; a buyback obligation that is not documented is not a commitment.

If the provider will not put the buyback quote in writing, or will not commit to the buyback structure contractually, or applies materially different buyback spreads to different products in the same account, that is information. What you do with the information is your decision.

## How Grace's buyback works in practice

When a Grace customer is ready to sell back, the process is straightforward.

The customer contacts their Grace advisor, through the same phone number or email they have used throughout the relationship. The advisor confirms the transaction details — ounces held, current spot price, the customer's instructions for the cash proceeds (distribution vs. rollover to another retirement account).

The spot price used is the London PM fix on the day the transaction is executed, sourced from a published third-party source. No markdown, no "liquidity adjustment," no "administrative reduction."

The metals are sold, the depository releases the physical gold to our transaction chain, the cash proceeds are transferred to the customer's custodian, and the custodian follows the customer's instructions for distribution or rollover.

The customer's advisor during the buyback transaction is salaried, just as on the entry side. There is no commission, no retention bonus, no incentive for the advisor to steer the customer toward any outcome other than the one the customer wants.

## What buyback-at-spot does not cover

Three clarifications.

**Custodian and depository fees on the exit side are separate.** Your custodian may charge a final-year account fee, and your depository may charge a partial-year storage fee; both are disclosed by those parties and do not go to Grace.

**The spot price is the market price on the day.** If the market spot price has risen since your purchase, you benefit. If the market spot price has fallen since your purchase, you absorb the loss. Grace's commitment is to buy at spot — it is not a commitment to guarantee the spot price or to guarantee any particular return on your holding. Gold prices move. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

**Certain physical-delivery scenarios carry logistics costs.** If a customer elects to take in-kind physical delivery rather than a cash buyback, shipping and insurance costs on the physical metal apply. These are disclosed at the point of the request.

## The summary

The exit spread is the more financially consequential number in a Gold IRA purchase, even though it is typically not shown at the point of entry. The industry pattern has been to generate a second margin on exit through a buyback spread below spot. Grace's structural commitment is to buy back at spot, with no markdown, as a design feature of the business rather than a promotional offer. The test for any Gold IRA provider, whether Grace or otherwise, is whether the buyback structure is published, contractually committed, and applied uniformly across the product catalogue.
