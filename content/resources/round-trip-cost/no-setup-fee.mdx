# Why "no setup fee" does not mean "no cost."

"No setup fee" is one of the more common lines in Gold IRA marketing. It appears on websites, in display ads, in radio spots. It is framed as a consumer benefit — you, the customer, save money because you are not being charged a setup fee.

Sometimes the claim is literally accurate. More often, it is structurally misleading, because the setup cost has not been eliminated — it has simply been moved.

This article explains how to read the claim, what to look for, and why the only reliable cost number is the round-trip cost.

## The structural options

A Gold IRA dealer has three structural options for recovering the cost of setting up and administering a new account.

**Option 1 — charge a visible setup fee.** A dealer says "we charge a $250 setup fee," and the customer pays that fee explicitly, on the invoice.

**Option 2 — absorb the setup cost into the entry spread.** A dealer says "no setup fee" and raises the entry markup enough to cover the setup cost. The customer does not see a setup fee line item, but they pay more on the spread than they would have without the setup cost being absorbed.

**Option 3 — absorb the setup cost into the buyback spread.** A dealer says "no setup fee" and keeps the entry markup competitive, but widens the buyback spread enough to cover the setup cost on the way out. The customer pays nothing at setup, pays a competitive entry cost, and then absorbs the setup cost at exit, years later.

All three options produce revenue for the dealer. All three are economically equivalent to the dealer, when sized correctly. But they produce different information for the customer. Options 2 and 3 produce a "no setup fee" marketing claim that is technically accurate but operationally identical to charging a setup fee — the customer pays the money, just in a different place.

## How to tell which option a dealer is using

You cannot tell from the "no setup fee" claim alone. The claim is consistent with all three options.

What you can do is evaluate the round-trip cost. If the dealer's entry markup and buyback spread, added together, produce a round-trip cost that is competitive with the category average, the "no setup fee" claim may be meaningful. If the dealer's round-trip cost is at or above the category average despite the "no setup fee" claim, the cost has been moved, not eliminated.

The round-trip cost is the only number that tells you this directly. Entry cost alone does not.

## Grace's approach

Grace's entry spread is 11.1%, all-in. That is the whole number. There is no separate setup fee. There is also no hidden setup fee absorbed into the spread, because the spread is the published spread — what it is, not what it is relative to some lower base figure we could have charged if we had charged a setup fee separately.

The buyback is at spot. No setup cost absorbed into the buyback. No exit cost at all.

The custodian and depository may each charge fees, which are disclosed by them directly and are not part of Grace's pricing.

## The summary

"No setup fee" is a claim to interrogate, not a claim to accept. The question to ask is: what is the round-trip cost? If the round-trip cost is competitive, the claim may be meaningful. If the round-trip cost is at or above category average, the setup cost has been moved, not eliminated.
