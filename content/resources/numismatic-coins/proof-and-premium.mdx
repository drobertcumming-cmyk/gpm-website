# What are "proof" and "premium" coins? And why the names don't match the math.

These are terms you will encounter if you have researched Gold IRAs. They appear in marketing materials, on dealer websites, in advisor conversations. This article explains what they technically mean, what the independent resale markets show they are actually worth, and why the gap matters.

## "Proof" coins — the technical definition

A "proof" coin, in the numismatic sense, is a coin struck with specially prepared dies and blanks, typically polished, producing a coin with a mirror-like finish and sharp design detail. Mints produce proof versions of standard bullion coins — proof American Eagles, proof Canadian Maple Leafs — alongside their regular issues.

Proof coins are priced by the issuing mint at a premium over bullion value to reflect the additional cost of production. This is a legitimate premium for the minting process itself.

**Where the math diverges:** a Gold IRA dealer selling a proof coin at the point of retail may price the coin at a premium far above the mint's own pricing, far above the premium at which the same coin trades in the independent numismatic market, and far above the buyback price at which the same dealer would repurchase the same coin from the customer a few years later. The mint's premium is real; the retail dealer's markup above the mint's premium is the gap.

## "Premium" coins — the marketing label

"Premium" is not a numismatic term. It is a marketing label used by Gold IRA retail dealers to position certain products as carrying value above bullion. Some products sold under the "premium" label do carry genuine numismatic value. Others do not — they are essentially standard bullion or proof coins with elevated marketing dressing, sold at markups well above the independent market value.

The independent test for whether a "premium" label is meaningful is simple: what does the same coin sell for in the broader numismatic market, and what would the same dealer who sold it to you pay to buy it back today? If the dealer's buyback price is close to bullion value, the "premium" was the dealer's margin, not the coin's market value.

## Why this matters for retirement-account decisions

A retirement account is held for years or decades. Across that period, a premium that was priced as permanent market value at the point of purchase, but that is priced as dealer margin at the point of buyback, produces a round-trip cost that is materially higher than the customer understood.

The question to ask at the point of purchase, in writing, is: what will you pay me to buy this specific coin back from me, today, at current spot prices? If the answer is close to the purchase price, the premium is likely to be durable. If the answer is close to bullion value, the premium is the dealer's margin.
