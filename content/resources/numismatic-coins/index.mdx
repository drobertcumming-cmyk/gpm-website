# We do not sell what this industry steers you into. Here is why.

Numismatic, "exclusive," "rare," "premium," and "proof" coins are the single most documented source of consumer harm in Gold IRA enforcement records. Grace's catalogue does not include any of them. This page is the pattern — what it is, why it exists, and why our refusal is a product-design decision rather than a marketing position.

## Definitions

First, what the terms mean.

**Standard bullion** refers to coins and bars whose value is based on the underlying weight of precious metal at current market prices. American Eagles, Canadian Maple Leafs, Austrian Philharmonics, South African Krugerrands, and LBMA-approved gold bars are standard bullion. They trade at a modest premium over spot — typically a few percent — reflecting minting costs and dealer margins, but the premium is bounded and the market value is the metal weight.

**Numismatic coins** are coins valued for their rarity, historical significance, condition, or collector interest rather than their metal weight. Genuine numismatic value is determined by scarcity, provenance, preservation, and collector demand, and it is a legitimate market segment served by specialized dealers, auctions, and grading services.

**"Premium," "exclusive," "rare," "proof," "limited-release," "specialty," or similar-labeled coins** are products sold in the Gold IRA retail channel that are positioned as having value above their metal content. Some of these products do carry genuine numismatic value. Others do not — they are marketed as carrying premium value, but their actual resale value, as measured by the independent numismatic market, is close to their underlying bullion value. The gap between the marketed value and the independent resale value is where the pattern of harm sits.

## The pattern

Documented in CFTC enforcement actions, state attorney general cases, and class-action settlements over the past two decades, the pattern runs as follows.

A Gold IRA customer — typically an older retiree in the preservation phase of their working life — contacts a Gold IRA dealer inquiring about rolling over a portion of their retirement account. The dealer's advisor — commissioned, not salaried — walks the customer through the decision. At some point in the conversation, the advisor introduces a "premium" or "exclusive" product category: coins that carry value above bullion, coins that the advisor presents as a superior hold for retirement purposes, coins that protect the customer from certain scenarios.

The markup on these products is substantially higher than the markup on standard bullion. In documented enforcement cases, markups of 30%, 50%, 80%, and in some cases above 100% above the underlying bullion value have been recorded. The customer, unfamiliar with the distinction between bullion value and numismatic value, purchases the product at the elevated price, understanding it to be a premium retirement asset.

Years later, the customer needs to sell. The dealer's buyback spread on the "premium" product is structured against the bullion value rather than the inflated purchase price. The customer, who thought they were selling a retirement asset worth the amount they paid, discovers that the actual resale value is the bullion weight at current spot price, less the buyback spread. The round-trip cost is the difference between the original purchase price and the buyback price, which on a high-markup premium product can approach or exceed 40% of the original purchase.

At the aggregate level, this pattern has been documented in CFTC enforcement actions involving hundreds of millions of dollars in consumer losses across the category over the past decade. The case files, the settlement amounts, and the individual victim accounts are a matter of public record.

## Why the pattern exists

Three structural factors.

**Commission-based advisor compensation.** When the advisor's income depends on margin generated, and margin on premium products is materially higher than margin on standard bullion, the economic incentive steers the conversation toward the higher-margin product. This is not a claim about any individual advisor's integrity; it is a structural observation about what compensation models produce in aggregate.

**Information asymmetry at the point of purchase.** The customer typically cannot, in the moment of the transaction, distinguish a coin whose premium reflects genuine numismatic scarcity from a coin whose premium reflects a marketing label. This requires specialist knowledge or independent valuation. By the time that knowledge can be acquired, the transaction is complete.

**Buyback structure on premium products.** When the buyback quote on a premium product is referenced to bullion value rather than to the original purchase price, the dealer's exit margin is determined by the gap between the two. A product sold at 80% above bullion, bought back at spot, produces an effective round-trip cost close to 80% — far beyond anything a reasonable customer would have expected from a "premium retirement asset."

## The regulatory response

Regulators have addressed this pattern through several mechanisms.

CFTC enforcement actions have targeted dealers whose premium coin sales practices fell outside anti-fraud thresholds. Settlement amounts have included restitution to affected customers and industry-wide bars or permanent injunctions against specific dealers.

State attorneys general have brought consumer protection actions under state unfair-trade-practice statutes, resulting in settlements and ongoing monitoring commitments.

Some states have passed or proposed statutes requiring specific disclosures at the point of Gold IRA sale, including the premium over bullion value for any coin sold as a retirement-account holding.

The FTC has issued consumer education advisories warning retirement-account customers about the distinction between bullion and numismatic products and about the specific marketing claims that have historically been associated with the pattern.

The pattern persists, however, because the structural factors that produce it — commission compensation, information asymmetry, buyback structure — remain in place across much of the category.

## Grace's structural refusal

Grace's product catalogue is standard IRS-eligible bullion only. We do not sell coins labeled as rare, exclusive, premium, proof, limited-release, specialty, or any other variant of those labels. Our advisors cannot steer you to a premium product because there is no premium product on the catalogue to steer you to. Our salaried-advisor compensation model means the advisor has no economic incentive to favor one product over another even if there were.

This is a published, permanent policy. It is not a promotional offer; it is the product-design foundation of the business.

The commercial consequence is that Grace earns less revenue per customer than a dealer with premium products on the catalogue. We have accepted that trade. The strategic position is that a Gold IRA company that is built around retirement-account customers should not have on its catalogue the product category most strongly associated with retirement-account consumer harm. Whatever the size of the industry's addressable market for premium coins, it is not a market we intend to serve.

## How to evaluate any Gold IRA provider on this dimension

Three questions, in writing, to any provider you are considering.

**"Does your catalogue include any products labeled as rare, exclusive, premium, proof, limited-release, or specialty?"**

**"If so, what is the typical premium above underlying bullion value for those products, and how is that premium substantiated?"**

**"What is your buyback policy on premium products? Is the buyback referenced to the original purchase price or to underlying bullion value?"**

A provider whose answers are clear, documented, and honest deserves consideration. A provider whose answers are vague, verbal-only, or evasive is giving you the information you need to decide.

Grace's refusal to sell these products is a structural design decision, not a marketing position. If you are evaluating any Gold IRA provider — Grace or otherwise — the three questions above are the load-bearing test.
