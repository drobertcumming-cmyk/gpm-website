# Standard bullion vs. numismatic: the cost difference in real numbers.

This article addresses the single most consequential pricing gap in the Gold IRA category: the difference between what customers pay for standard bullion and what customers pay for numismatic or "premium" coins.

## Standard bullion — what the premium looks like

Standard bullion coins and bars trade at a premium over spot that reflects minting costs and dealer margins. For coins, the typical retail premium in the Gold IRA channel runs in a range — widely documented in industry data — of roughly 3%–15% above spot, depending on the product, the dealer, and market conditions. For bars, the premium is typically lower, reflecting the simpler production.

At Grace, the published spread of 11.1% is the all-in premium over spot for our standard bullion catalogue. That is the whole number.

## Numismatic and "premium" — what the premium looks like

Documented retail markups on numismatic, proof, and "premium" coin products in the Gold IRA channel have ranged widely — from modest premiums reflecting genuine numismatic value in the 15%–30% range, to aggressive markups in the 50%–100% range, to egregious markups above 100% documented in CFTC enforcement cases.

The wide range reflects two things. First, some numismatic products are genuinely scarce, and their premiums reflect actual collector market pricing. Second, some products labeled as "premium" in the retail Gold IRA channel are not scarce in the broader numismatic market, and the premium is the dealer's margin rather than a market fact.

## The cost difference, in dollars

Consider two hypothetical customers, each rolling over $150,000 into a Gold IRA.

**Customer A** is sold standard bullion at an 11.1% premium over spot. Entry cost: $16,650. Buyback at spot on exit: zero exit spread. Round-trip dealer revenue: $16,650.

**Customer B** is sold "premium" coins at a 60% markup over underlying bullion. Entry cost: $90,000. Buyback at underlying bullion value less a 10% buyback spread: exit receives approximately $54,000 on the bullion basis against the $150,000 originally paid. Round-trip dealer revenue, effectively: $96,000.

This example is illustrative, not attributed to any specific dealer. But the structural arithmetic is the arithmetic the CFTC has documented across enforcement cases in the category.

## Why the gap exists

Three factors, discussed at length in the pillar article: commissioned advisor compensation that creates an economic incentive to sell higher-margin products, information asymmetry that prevents most retail customers from distinguishing genuine numismatic value from dealer markup at the point of purchase, and buyback structures referenced to bullion rather than to purchase price.

At Grace, none of the three factors is present. Advisors are salaried. The catalogue is bullion only. The buyback is at spot across the catalogue.
